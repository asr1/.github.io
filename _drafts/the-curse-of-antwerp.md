Once the chanting died down we found sleep, and woke before the city. We had a train to catch. After a quick breakfast, we stopped by reception and asked if they could call us a cab to the train station. 

No problem, she said. 

Fifteen minutes later she asked why we were still there. The cab hadn't come (what other reason could we offer?). She called again, rapidly speaking in Flemish. She delivered the news: the taxis aren't running today. Everyone is recovering (read: hungover) from last night's festivities. 

Oh no.

If we'd been told that from the start, we might have been fine. As it was, we had to cross town on short notice. We ran, our small suitcases thudding over each and every cobblestone that lined the road. I checked my watch and the map constantly, aware that it would come down to minutes. 

We reached the gorgeous facade of the station with minutes to spare. The only thing that loomed between us and our destination was a ladder, spread across the sidewalk, stretching to heights unknown. I slammed to a stop, preparing to divert around the terrible omen. "There's no time!" my wife shouted, pushing me through, both of us passing directly beneath the hungry maw of that terrible omen.

This is where our troubles began.

## Antwerp

We crossed the threshold of the station into a scene of chaos. The escalators were out of order. We needed to cross the station, and the only way to do it was to descend down stairs, suitcases growing larger and heavier in our minds with every step, each souvenir and trinket mocking us for our greed, and then back up the other side, thump thump thump. 

We rush to the train, and the doors close in front of us. I look left, the doors are still open.

You know that scene in every spy movie where the protagonists walk down a dark hallway, lights following them each step of the way? It was like that, but with closing train doors. We raced. My wife stumbled. Later, she'd claim I stepped on the back of her shoe, gave her a flat tire. Aside from the inverted NEW BALANCE logo imprinted into the remains of her footwear, no evidence supports this theory. 

I arrive moments before her (see above re: stumble) at the final door. An old lady is climbing on to the train before us, pulling herself up, and balancing in the doorway for an eternity. Were she younger or sprightlier we may have pushed past her. Instead we hemmed and hoped until she pulled ahead. Like a gas, we immediately filled the space as she left it, the hissing of the door punctuating our entrance. We made it.

We exchanged a sigh for a seat and collapsed into relief, catching our breath. 

We arrived at Charles de Gaul airport 10 minutes behind schedule.

Our troubles were just beginning.

## The Worst Airport in Europe

The train took us to Charles De Gaul airport, a place that should be named after a worse person, as their aspirations fall short of his particular legacy. They should call it "Third best baker on Champs-Élysées airport" or "guy I once heard about in history class but don't remember why he's significant airport, maybe Marc Bloch or Henri Grégoire airport". It's a mouthful, certainly, but it'll get referred to by its IATA code anyway.

What follows is a series of errors, each less probable than the one before.

First, another broken elevator, but this time we needed to go up from our platform into the airport proper. A large elevator sat in the middle of the walkway, but we were slightly too slow, and we watched the crowd fill the giant glass box before us. No problem, we said. It's just one floor, and we're first in line.

The elevator took almost five minutes to make its round trip, and by the time it finished, a small crowd had gathered behind us. No problem. The doors slid open... and railway attendants wheeled in a woman more ancient than the land from our right. They pushed her in, slowly, creeping, to immediately before the doors, gently nestled her within the great yawning elevator... and then it closed, with no opportunity for us, or anyone else to board alongside her. 

A check of the watch, we couldn't spare another five minutes. Up the stairs, suitcases clanging, growing heavier with each agonizing step.

 The first check-in kiosk we tried to use was broken. The second was out of toner, and printed blank sheets. The third insisted we had already checked in, didn't we have our blank sheets? Each trip to the kiosks required waiting in line anew, until we finally explained to an agent how the kiosks were conspiring against us. 

It was at this moment that I first mentioned the ladder, but my wife was in no mood to hear about the cause of our consternation. Regardless, we had our tickets. 

The security line is on the other side of the airport. The escalators are broken here as well. We are mere feet from our destination, but must circle the airport to get there. The stairs are nowhere near the escalators, and elevators won't be invented in France for another 50 years. 

My wife goes to customer service (long line) to figure out where we need to go. I ask a passerby, utilizing my handy-dandy French/English phrasebook. He replies "Follow me" (in French) and I panic, trying to get my wife's attention. She's in line, facing away from me. I panic, and shout "UN MOMENTO" at the nice man. I grab my wife, and when I return, he is crestfallen, and speaking perfect English. I attempt to express the depths of my regret, but have only my face with which to do it. He guides us to the checkpoint.

The line is long. Long and winding. Signs along the way ask if we've had an important delay. They are in French, of course, and earn chuckles from a flock of American schoolchildren. [^1]

The security line was long. So long. We expressed concern that we would miss our flight. A nice man in front of us offered to let us pass him. I can't remember if we accepted or not, as one position would make very little difference, but we continued to chat with this gentleman as the line snailed forward. 

I'm frantically checking my watch, repeating the mantra. We're not going to make it. We're not going to make it. Finally, a ray of hope: an agent walks through the line "If anyone is going to miss their flight, come this way!" The gentleman behind us calls out THESE TWO WILL! OVER HERE! I thank him and rush forward. The woman looks at our tickets and says, "Oh you have 15 minutes, you're fine." (????) She sends us back to our place in line, allowing other passengers to skip the queue. 

3 minutes before boarding closes. I get through security. I start to run. I stop. My wife's bag has been flagged. They open her bag. It's full, absolutely laded with Belgian chocolate. You know that scene in every crime movie where someone opens a suitcase lined with gold bars, you couldn't fit another one in there? It was like that. But with chocolate. [^2]:

The agent sighed and closed her suitcase, waving us through. We sprint. I see the gate. I see the gate agents. I see the gate agents closing the gate. We are 10, 15 seconds too late. I pant, tell her this is my flight. She says she has to call the plane, they aren't supposed to re-open the door. I say it's been 10 seconds. She agrees and makes the call. 

There is nothing she can do. She sends me to customer service. They can help me get on the next flight, she says. She points me to a station. It's at least nearby.

## The rudest agent in all of Europe

The line for customer service is short, but the man at the counter has no time for me. I explain the situation: our train got in late, security line was long, we missed our flight by...well, by so little time that it hasn't left yet, it's there, right behind you, with two empty seats. 

The man turns his nose up at me. Oh, you should have planned better, should have taken an earlier train. I can sell you the same flight tomorrow, $1500 each. If you had been late because of us, well, that would be a different story, we take care of our customers, but this is your mistake, you see.

Sir, I booked a combined train+plane ticket, the train was part of the ticket.

It was third party, you should have taken an earlier one. If we had cause the delay, we could help you for free, but you simply must pay. If you had been a first class customer, we would help you, but you booked economy, so there is nothing we can do.

No, I booked the train through CDG!

Sir, this is an airport.

I had never heard as much contempt as he put into this word. He continued explaining to me that I had chosen to be cheap and manage my time poorly while I frantically frumbled through my phone looking for proof. Finally, I found it: a train+plane ticket, which, to my delight, listed the first leg of the journey as:

DEPARTING: ANT
ARRIVING: CDG
AIRCRAFT TYPE: TRAIN

Immediately the man's attitude changed. "Oh, sir, let me make a call, we will take care of you." 

Suddenly we had options, and none of them cost $1500 a ticket. 
Unfortunately, these options involved leaving security, crossing to a different terminal, and re-entering security. But we had time to do it. Our flight would leave in 6 hours.

## Six hours in Charles de Gaul

We clear customs a second time. The agent asks us if we have any dairy products. 

My wife informs him that she has a lot of chocolate, does that count?

I'm asking about dairy he says.

Some of it's milk chocolate?

DAIRY!  

Ok, okay. No dairy.  

Good. Do you have any fruit?  

Well, some of the chocolate has fruit—  

DO YOU KNOW WHAT A FRUIT IS? 

The man had no respect for our caprcious American ways of classification.

### The Curse

The gate we were to fly out of was self-contained. By that, I mean there was very little to do there. A handful of chairs, a Starbucks, and a bathroom. 

I googled how to undue the ladder curse thing, and learned that the best way is to spit through the rungs of the ladder you walked through. Well, that was no longer an option. What else you got?

Spit on your shoe, it says, and don't look at it until it dries. No problem. I bent down in the vestibule of the bathroom, pretending to tie my shoe. I let out some spit, and looked away. Now I just had to wait.

### The Food

Hunger found me. 

I waited in line at the Starbucks. My wife asked me to buy her a cookie and a drink. No problem. I gathered my fare, and went to pay. 

My card decliend. It had been working fine for almost two weeks, but it knew I couldn't possibly be trying to spend this much money (much less time!) in Charles De Gaul airport.

I tried it again, same result. I tried my spare card. No dice. Okay, okay. I pull out my money, I have a little bit of cash left. Some Euros. It's not enough. I push the cookie aside (still wrapped, mind). The cashier pushes it back. She doesn't understand. 

No, I cannot afford all of this. I'm less than a euro short. I push it aside, she pushes it back. I gesture to my money, she gestures to my food. We repeat this dance until finally I make her understand. I pay, and now I have money no more.

Later, I would find in my pocket another 3 or 4 euro in coins that easily would have covered the difference.

## The Flight

Our initial route was direct to Seattle, but this detour had us going through JFK, a miserable place with multiple terminals that are not connected to each other, populated by smiling cretins who will gladly tell you the wrong directions to get where you're going. We went through security three times trying to find our gate. The first two were in the wrong building, circling endlessly like we'd discovered a new circle of hell. [^3]

We got to our gate and I found a McDonalds. I placed my order, and my wife's and the cashier holds up a strawberry milkshake as I turn to walk away. Did you want this? She asks. Oh, I didn't order that. I know, she says. Do you want it?

I take the milkshake, dancing all the way back to our seats. I'm naturally a lucky person[^4], and I knew it was back. I distrbuted the good news along with the food, and my wife scoffs. That's gross, she says. I'm not doing it. 

It worked! 

Nope. She says. This is silly gross superstition. She'd said the same thing at CDG, and even in the face of this proof refused to see reason. 

Oh well.

We fly back to rainy, cozy, lovely Seattle, waving at the Space Needle as we descend. 

The next day, my wife, almost convinced, decides to play a little trick. She spits on her shoes as she leaves for work... wearing a different pair entirely. That'll teach them! A loophole!

She was laid off later that day.

## Aftermath

Sometimes I tell people that my friends would describe me as "a little superstitious". If any of my friends are nearby, they are quick to jump in and say that they wouldn't qualify it, or if they wouldn't it wouldn't be in the dimminutive direciton. Whatever.

My wife, she's not a believer, not in this. But after what happened in Antwerp, she's not a doubter anymore either.
